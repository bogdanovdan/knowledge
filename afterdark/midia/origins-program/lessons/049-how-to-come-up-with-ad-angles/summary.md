## Overview
This lesson breaks down how to generate advertising angles by connecting them directly to customer sub-avatars rather than internal test ideas or isolated product features. Designed for Shopify brand operators and creative strategists, the session clarifies the fundamental distinction between ad concepts and angles, establishes why angles exist on a spectrum, and demonstrates a manual 3-step extraction method. Finally, the instructor demonstrates an AI-assisted workflow using Claude to generate ranked angles, actionable "reasons to buy," and direct hooks/headlines ready for creative testing.

## Key teachings
- Most advertisers make weak ads because their definition of an "angle" focuses on what they want to test rather than how to position the product to the customer [00:01:28].
- Concepts are internally focused test ideas (formats, iterations, broad strategies), while angles are externally focused and provide the customer's specific reason to buy [00:00:52], [00:07:03], [00:09:43].
- Formats like Post-it note ads or "3 reasons why" fail if the angle inside the format is poor; the copy and angle matter more than the visual delivery format [00:04:13], [00:05:18].
- Concepts and angles overlap and are used interchangeably only when the concept specifically is to test an angle (e.g., "Test Buy It For Life"), but they are not the same thing 100% of the time [00:07:55], [00:08:32].
- Best practice rule: For every ad concept you write down, always explicitly state what the angle is (the exact reason a customer should buy) to ensure clarity [00:08:53], [00:11:04].
- Angles exist on a spectrum from broad to specific; broad core avatars produce broad, weak angles (e.g., "Want better sleep?"), whereas sub-avatars naturally generate specific, actionable angles [00:12:14], [00:13:20], [00:16:05].
- Traditional marketing starts with product features, jumps straight to benefits, and creates broad, generic angles that fail 99% of the time [00:16:35], [00:17:22].
- Top-performing creative strategy moves backwards: Product -> Desires -> Core Avatar -> Sub-Avatars -> Angles -> Direct Hooks/Headlines [00:17:42], [00:18:27].
- A hook/headline is the vehicle used to communicate the angle; junior writers should write direct hooks instead of indirect ones until reaching advanced stages [00:20:34], [00:21:52].
- Angles must remain actionable; an actionable angle directly sets up clear copy and makes ad creation significantly easier [00:23:42], [00:29:16].
- When testing generated angles, you do not need to test every angle separately; test the #1 hook carrying the highest emotional weight first, or layer secondary angles into the body copy [00:28:37], [00:32:50].

## Frameworks and models

### Concept vs. Angle Framework
- **Concept (Internally focused / About your test strategy):**
  - "I want to test a video version" (No buying reason = Identify the angle) [00:09:54].
  - "I want to do an iteration" (Iteration of what? = Identify the angle) [00:10:06].
  - "Us vs. Them" (Comparing what? = Concept with an assumed comparison angle) [00:02:39], [00:10:42].
  - "Before & After" (Transformation of what? = Concept with an assumed transformation angle) [00:03:30].
- **Angle (Externally focused / Customer's reason to buy):**
  - "Stops tossing and turning" (Solves a specific problem) [00:10:26].
  - "Brings back your energy" (Direct customer outcome) [00:10:51].
  - Rule: *If the concept doesn't give the customer a reason to buy, it's not an angle yet* [00:09:43].

### 3-Step Sub-Avatar Angle Extraction
1. **Identify the Desire:** Determine what the sub-avatar wants (e.g., restorative sleep) [00:24:43].
2. **Identify the Behavior:** Observe what the sub-avatar currently does to satisfy that desire (e.g., wears nose strips to bed) [00:24:57].
3. **Identify the Gap:** Spot the unmet need between their current behavior and their ultimate desire to form the angle (e.g., they wear nose strips but still want restorative sleep -> Angle: *"Better than nose strips"* or *"This combined with nose strips..."*) [00:25:06], [00:26:03].

### Direct Hook Structures (From Proven Ad Patterns)
- **Solution Exaggeration / Timeline + Outcome:** Pair a concrete timeframe with problem elimination (e.g., *"14+ Hours. No Swelling"* or *"No screens for 1 hour. No light for 8 hours"*) [00:21:00], [00:29:49].
- **This is Not the Product / Elevation:** Frame the product beyond its normal category (e.g., *"This is a meal. Not a protein shake"* or *"This isn't a curtain. This is a sleep protocol"*) [00:21:20], [00:31:40].
- **Speed + Problem Elimination:** Combine rapid setup with an emotional or behavioral outcome (e.g., *"5 minutes to install. No more snapping"* or *"5 minutes to install. 0% of your discipline wasted"*) [00:21:45], [00:31:07].
- **Accidental Ideal Outcome:** Use curiosity and unusual numbers to build believability (e.g., *"I Accidentally Lost 12 Pounds At 52"*) [00:22:23], [00:23:00].
- **Direct Avatar Callout:** Call out the specific pain of the sub-avatar immediately (e.g., *"Teen Always Grumpy?"*) [00:23:16].

## Numbers, benchmarks and rules of thumb
- **99% of the time:** Ads fail when built from the conventional path of product -> feature -> benefit -> generic angle [00:17:22].
- **Law of Unique Numbers:** Using non-round numbers in hooks (e.g., "12 pounds", "52", "56.4%") increases believability compared to round estimates like 10 pounds or 50% [00:23:00].
- **$100,000 per day:** Peak ad spend referenced by the instructor using this avatar-to-angle methodology [00:34:16].
- **3:2:2 method:** Dynamic creative testing format mentioned for deploying three generated hooks [00:32:53].
