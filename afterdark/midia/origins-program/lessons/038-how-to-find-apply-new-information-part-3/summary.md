## Overview
This lesson teaches e-commerce founders and marketers how to uncover and apply "new information" to direct response advertising without needing a completely novel product. The instructor explains how to position a brand as an educational authority, combine new information with unique mechanisms, use AI prompts to identify recent scientific breakthroughs, and refine ad hooks to avoid high-intensity fearmongering.

## Key teachings
- Finding new information allows brands to position themselves as trusted authorities by educating consumers about problems and solutions they were not previously aware of [00:01:19].
- New information does not require an entirely unique product, though combining new information with a new mechanism provides the biggest performance lift [00:00:15].
- AI research must be manually fact-checked and verified; consumers are hyper-educated, and running unverified or false claims damages brand credibility [00:02:01].
- Claims must logically align with the product type being sold (e.g., a brand selling mushroom powder cannot claim that powder form destroys active compounds) [00:03:34].
- Hook copy should avoid technical jargon that confuses viewers, following the principle of simplicity (e.g., Apple's "1,000 songs in your pocket" rather than device specs) [00:11:44].
- Marketers must not fearmonger or deploy low-valence, high-intensity hooks (e.g., framing light exposure as a "brain health emergency"), which can trigger ad account bans or platform removal by Meta and Shopify [00:13:38, 00:15:29].
- If an AI prompt outputs hooks that feel overly alarmist, marketers must explicitly instruct the tool to lower emotional intensity and pivot toward educational, positive benefit framing [00:16:11, 00:18:02].
- A foundational filter for ethical copy is whether the marketer would feel comfortable running that exact message to their own mother or sister [00:19:12].

## Frameworks and models

### The Valence and Intensity Emotional Scale [00:15:54]
Used to assess the ethical risk and emotional impact of advertising hooks:
- **Valence (Direction of Emotion)**:
  - *High Valence*: Positive emotional tone.
  - *Low Valence*: Negative emotional tone (fear, anxiety, concern).
- **Intensity (Degree of Emotion)**:
  - Measures how strongly positive or negative the messaging is.
  - *Rule*: While low-valence angles can be used, entering the high-intensity / low-valence quadrant (extreme fearmongering) destroys brand trust, attracts low-quality customers, and risks policy bans [00:18:27].

### Eugene Schwartz's 5 Stages of Market Sophistication [00:08:40, 00:08:55]
- *Stage 1 (First to Market)*: Simple, direct benefit statements suffice.
- *Stage 2 (Competition Emerges)*: Amplified benefit claims and superlatives compete for attention.
- *Stage 3 (Market Saturation)*: Audiences grow skeptical; brands require unique mechanisms or proprietary processes explaining *how* products work.
- *Stage 4 (Advanced Sophistication)*: New discoveries, breakthrough research, or expanded benefits are essential to break through fatigue.
- *Stage 5 (Complete Fatigue)*: Requires complete repositioning or identifying entirely new markets.

### Claude New Information Prompt Execution Workflow [00:08:20]
1. Upload the reference documents (*Market Sophistication Document* and the copied *New Information Prompt*).
2. Input the target product details:
   - Product Name
   - Product URL
3. Instruct the model to surface recent research (published within the last 12–24 months) structured by:
   - Source (publication and date)
   - Why it is new (market sophistication gap)
   - How to use ethically (educational positioning)
   - 3 marketing hook samples focused on curiosity and education rather than fearmongering
4. Review findings, audit for emotional intensity, and prompt revisions to soften alarmist copy into constructive education.
5. Manually verify research claims outside AI before creating creative assets.
